1. Fundamental Factors that Influenced Change in Southeast Asia (pp. 34-42)

Three fundamental factors shaped the rise and fall of states in Southeast Asia: Topography, Humans, and Resources.

1.1 Topography (pp. 34-37)

Southeast Asia is divided into two geographical types: Mainland (a continuous landmass) and Maritime/Island Southeast Asia. Malaysia is unusual in having territory in both categories — Peninsular Malaysia on the mainland, and Sarawak/Sabah on the island of Borneo.

  • On the mainland, complex mountain ranges run north to south, with major rivers (like the Mekong River, 4,909 km long — the 12th longest river in the world) originating from the Tibetan Plateau region near the Himalayas.
  • The Malay Peninsula (part of Maritime SEA) has only short streams and rivers, and is separated from the rest of the mainland by the Strait of Malacca.
  • Climate: Southeast Asia is generally hot and tropical, with an average temperature of 18-25°C — very well suited to human settlement. Monsoon winds cause the wet/dry seasons: winds from Siberia and South China bring cold, dry weather (roughly Feb-May), while winds from the Indian Ocean bring moisture (roughly May-Sep).
  • Earthquakes and volcanoes: many islands sit on the Ring of Fire in the western Pacific, causing frequent earthquakes and volcanic eruptions — Indonesia and the Philippines are affected most often. While dangerous, volcanic activity also fertilizes the soil, supporting farming and dense settlement, and sheltered coastlines create favorable conditions for maritime travel and trade.
  • Islands: Borneo (Kalimantan) is shared by three countries — Indonesia (largest share), Malaysia (Sarawak and Sabah), and Brunei. The Philippines is the easternmost country, with about 7,000 islands, while Indonesia has even more — around 10,000 large and small islands (archipelagos). The Philippines also faces at least 5 typhoons every year, since its rainy season brings storms from both the Pacific and Indian Oceans.

1.2 Humans (p. 38)

According to the "Out of Africa" Theory, all humans originally migrated from West Africa to other parts of the world, including Southeast Asia. There were two main reasons people kept moving and migrating:

  • Settlement — to find a better place for food and living, such as access to water, fertile soil, minerals, forests, and agricultural/fishery resources.
  • Political and trade expansion — to expand power, wealth, and stability. This drive eventually led to trade competition, wars, and attempts to monopolize valuable goods — a pattern that repeats throughout the rest of this unit, from ancient trading kingdoms to European colonization.

1.3 Resources (pp. 39-42) expanded

Southeast Asia's natural wealth was one of the biggest reasons it attracted traders, settlers, and — later — colonizers.

  • Fertile volcanic soil — the same volcanic activity that causes earthquakes also enriches the soil with minerals, making it ideal for intensive rice farming and supporting a dense population.
  • Spices — cloves, nutmeg, and pepper (especially from the "Spice Islands" in present-day Indonesia) were extremely valuable and rare in the ancient world. Demand for these spices was one of the main reasons Chinese, Indian, Arab, and later European traders sailed to the region.
  • Minerals — tin (especially from the Malay Peninsula) and gold were mined and traded for centuries.
  • Marine resources — fish, pearls, and access to major sea trade routes between India and China made coastal areas valuable.
  • Strategic location as a resource — controlling the Strait of Malacca meant controlling one of the world's busiest trade chokepoints, connecting the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea.

Why this matters for the rest of Unit 2: these same resources explain why ancient trading kingdoms like Funan, Srivijaya, and Malacca (Section 2) grew rich and powerful, and why Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, and Britain later competed to colonize the region for spices, tin, and rubber (Section 3).

2. Ancient States & Kingdoms, and Beliefs & Religions (pp. 42-58)

Historical transitions in Southeast Asia can be divided into 3 periods: 2.1 Ancient States & Kingdoms (pp.42-58), 2.2 Western Colonization (pp.59-71), and 2.3 Post-WWII Independent States (pp.72-83).

2.1 Ancient States and Kingdoms

Large city-state communities existed from the Iron Age, usually forming around water sources. Kingdoms are classified by location of origin:

RegionKingdoms
WestSri Ksetra, Mon (in present-day Myanmar)
CenterDvaravati
EastFunan, Champa, Ancient Khmer, Lan Xang
IslandsSrivijaya, Malacca, states on Java island
  • Bagan (Myanmar, West) is home to more than 2,500 Buddhist stupas, temples, and monasteries — the largest and densest concentration of Buddhist monuments in the world.
  • Vietnam (East) developed into a state around the 3rd century B.E., was later occupied by China, expanded further, then was colonized by France near the end of the 24th century B.E.
  • Ancient Khmer (East, the Angkor era) was established by King Jayavarman II, centered in Siem Reap. It grew to control territory from the Mekong River Delta and southern Laos, across the Mun-Chi River Basin, to the Chao Phraya-Tha Chin River Basin — one of the largest ancient kingdoms in the region.
  • Srivijaya (Islands) was a powerful maritime trading empire.

2.2 Beliefs and Religions in Southeast Asia

Local beliefs existed first, before any organized religion arrived. According to the unit's key takeaway, Buddhism actually arrived before Brahmanism-Hinduism, even though they are often discussed in a different order.

Local beliefs

  • An abstract concept involving natural and supernatural powers, represented concretely through symbols and idols.
  • Rituals are performed to ask for help and protection from supernatural powers.
  • Because Southeast Asian people are closely tied to family systems and agriculture, beliefs often center on the worship of ancestors and natural forces.
  • Old and new beliefs are frequently blended together — for example, respect for the Naga is common among Thai, Laotian, and Cambodian people; in Myanmar, spirits are worshipped alongside Buddhism today; and the Minangkabau tribe in Indonesia still holds funerals in the same fashion as ancient times.

Brahmanism-Hinduism

  • Originated in India about 5,000 years ago.
  • Has a large number of gods, including the Trimurti (three major gods).
  • Its ultimate goal is to break free from the cycle of reincarnation and reunite with the Absolute Soul (Brahman), guided by karma.
  • Entered Southeast Asia later than Buddhism did.
  • The ruling class of Southeast Asian states accepted Brahmanism-Hinduism because it elevated the ruler's status to that of a god-king, which helped promote stability and legitimacy for the reign and royal succession.

Buddhism

  • Originated in India and spread into many cultures, blending with indigenous gods and beliefs — giving rise to the Bodhisattva concept within Mahayana Buddhism.
  • Rulers of Java, Dvaravati, Bagan, and the Khmer Empire accepted Mahayana Buddhism during some historical periods.
  • Vietnam is the only Southeast Asian population that practices Mahayana Buddhism, which was conveyed to it from China.
  • Theravada Buddhism played a major role starting in the 18th century B.E., supported by kings of the time, who used the concept of the Ten Royal Virtues to strengthen their connection with citizens.

Islam

  • Originated in the Arabian Peninsula in the year 622 AD; worships only one god.
  • Separated into 3 sects: Sunni, Shia, and Sufi.
  • Spread into Southeast Asia through trade with Indian traders (from South India) who had already converted to Islam.
  • Arabian, Persian, and Moor traders — who monopolized profits from east-west trade — tried to build a trading empire spanning from the Bay of Bengal to the South China Sea and the Spice Islands, exploiting power struggles among rulers in Java and Sumatra.
  • Local rulers eventually converted to Islam and established Islamic states.

Christianity

  • Spread from Palestine and Turkey into Europe, centered at the Vatican; the Pope is the highest leader of the Church.
  • Spread from Europe to Asia as a result of the discovery of new shipping routes in the 20th century B.E.
  • Two Southeast Asian states converted to Christianity: the Philippines and South Vietnam.

3. The Colonial Period and Japanese Occupation (pp. 59-71)

3.1 Reasons behind Western occupation (p. 59)

Goods from Asia were extremely popular among Europeans, but the Silk Road trade route was dominated by Muslim traders (especially after the Crusades). Western Europeans wanted to break free from Muslim and Italian traders, who had grown wealthy controlling the sale of Asian goods. There were two periods of occupation, each with different objectives, countries involved, and policies:

PeriodLeading powersMain motives
21st-22nd century B.E.Portugal and SpainSearch for spices/Asian goods, spread of Christianity, struggle for a share of profits
23rd-25th century B.E.Britain and FranceThe Industrial Revolution's demand for resources/markets, and political competition among Western powers
  • Spain sailed west across the Atlantic (based on the spherical earth theory), discovered America, then crossed the Pacific into Asia by 2064 B.E. Facing difficulty accessing spice sources directly, Spain instead colonized the Philippines and set up port cities as trading stations.
  • Portugal sailed south along the West African coast to India, Southeast Asia, and China, successfully controlling the spice trade.
  • By the 21st century B.E., Spain had replaced Portugal as the most prominent maritime power; Britain, France, and the Netherlands then competed to take a share of Spain's profits. By the 22nd century B.E., Britain and France were focused on India, leaving the Netherlands to take control of trade in the East India Islands (Indonesia).
  • In the 24th century B.E., Britain (advancing from the west via Myanmar) and France (advancing from the east via Indochina) competed for Southeast Asia, with Thailand acting as a buffer state between them. The Netherlands needed British support, so it allowed Britain to expand into the Strait of Malacca. Meanwhile, Spain lost the Philippines to the USA.

3.2 Western occupation by country (p. 62)

PowerApproach and territories
PortugalOccupied only strategic port cities as trading hubs (a more diplomatic approach) — e.g. Malacca (natural resources; later lost to the Netherlands after conflict with the local Muslim ruler) and Macao (trading hub). Portugal also left Timor Leste, whose income later came mainly from petroleum extraction by Australia.
SpainOccupied the Philippines, built Manila as the government center and Luzon Island as a trading hub with China. Locals were taxed and forced to pay tribute; Britain later forced Spain to make Manila a free port. Filipino nationalists eventually joined forces with the US.
USATook control of the Philippines after the Spanish-American War. Although local nationalists hoped the US would grant them liberty, the US instead colonized the Philippines and suppressed the nationalist movement, forcing locals to adopt international/Western values.
NetherlandsThe private Dutch trading company (VOC) began trade, later backed fully by the government. It eliminated Portuguese influence in Malacca and expanded control over Java and Sumatra, forcing locals into an agricultural system that eventually caused widespread local hatred.
BritainFocused on India, Southeast Asian port cities, and expansion into China. British colonies included the Malay Peninsula (Strait of Malacca), other Malay states, northern Borneo (Kalimantan) states, and Myanmar. Burma was first governed via the Governor of India, then became a direct British colony after WWI.
FranceInitially sought closer ties with Siam, but this was short-lived due to Siam's internal political turmoil. France then competed with Britain and took control of three major states — Vietnam, Khmer (Cambodia), and Laos — together known as French Indochina.

3.3 Nationalist movements and the demand for liberation (p. 68) expanded

Nationalist movements developed in two periods: Pre-Pacific War and the Pacific War.

CountryNationalist movement
MyanmarAfter WWI, Britain allowed Myanmar to govern itself semi-independently from India. During WWII, General Aung San — remembered today as the "Father of the Nation" — approached Japan for assistance toward Burmese liberation, later founding the resistance movement that became the AFPFL. He was assassinated in July 1947, just six months before Burma's independence on 4 January 1948.
The Malay statesBritain administered the Malay states relatively well, so people enjoyed stability and prosperity at the time. As a result, nationalism here focused mainly on preserving ethnic, linguistic, and Islamic identity, rather than demanding full independence immediately. (After the war, the United Malays National Organisation, UMNO, formed in 1946 to represent Malay political interests.)
IndonesiaThe nationalist movement started in Java and expanded to Sumatra, focusing on economic problems, anti-Chinese and anti-Dutch sentiment, and demands for independence. Key leaders Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta declared Indonesia's independence on 17 August 1945.
The PhilippinesThe nationalist movement began during Spanish colonial rule and continued through the US occupation period. The Philippines was allowed to self-govern (as a Commonwealth) before achieving full independence.
IndochinaIn Vietnam, the movement involved violent resistance across all social classes (led by figures such as Ho Chi Minh, who founded the Viet Minh in 1941). In Laos and Khmer (Cambodia), the nationalist movement was much less active at this stage.

3.4 Japanese Occupation during World War II (p. 70)

  • Japan invaded Manchuria and northern China, and entered the Axis Powers alongside Germany and Italy.
  • Japan's purpose was to take advantage of Southeast Asia's resources for military supplies — especially rice, oil, tin, and rubber.
  • Japan expressed sympathy with nationalist movements, gaining their cooperation during the Pacific War. Japan considered itself the most powerful country in Asia during WWII because it could invade China, it was an ally of the Axis Powers, and it promoted the slogan of the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere."
  • Japan's governing policy expressed sympathy with major nationalist movements and supported their operations from a command center in Singapore, covering Myanmar, the Philippines, Indonesia, Indochina, and the Strait of Malacca — but in practice, Japan often had conflict with local governments.
  • Militarily, Japan divided its occupied zones into two: the Army controlled more developed, densely populated areas, while the Navy controlled less-developed islands, including those in the South Pacific. Japan promised to grant eventual independence and preserve local social structures, institutions, and customs — though this promise was not always honored in practice.

3.5 Impacts of WWII and Japanese Occupation (p. 71)

AreaImpact
PoliticsMany Southeast Asian countries eventually turned back to former colonial powers to help resist and push out Japan.
EconomicsThe population suffered wartime hardship, including inflation, poverty, scarcity, and shortages of agricultural products — because these were forced to be sold for Japan's military consumption.
SocietySevere destruction of towns and transportation channels; hardship, injuries, deaths, and family separation from bombing; no livelihoods or shelters; changes in social status; and broader quality-of-life problems such as orphaned children, crime, prostitution, and slum communities.

4. Independent States after World War II

After WWII, the world split into two ideological camps: liberal democratic states versus socialist communist states — the beginning of the Cold War. This divide shaped how each Southeast Asian country's path to independence unfolded.

Myanmar

Aung San Suu Kyi became the leader of the Opposition Party in Myanmar. After gaining liberation from the British in 2491 B.E., Myanmar has faced ongoing political instability, military rule, limited freedoms, and human rights violations.

Singapore

Singapore separated from Malaysia because of differences in ethnicity and beliefs. Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew (who served for 31 years) developed the country into one of the most important international port cities, a center of petroleum refining and technology industries, backed by strict laws and policies.

Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia)

The Battle of Dien Bien Phu ended in France's defeat, and Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia gained liberation — but the region could not develop quickly because Indochina became a Cold War battlefield (the Vietnam War).

  • Laos faced internal conflict between a constitutional monarchy led by the Laotian king and socialist communism led by Prince Souphanouvong.
  • Cambodia: King Norodom Sihanouk cut diplomatic ties with the US. Around 20 years later, a communist party led by Pol Pot (the Khmer Rouge) rose to power.

Indonesia and Timor Leste

Sukarno, the national hero of Indonesia, fought the Dutch and became the country's first elected president. Later, Major General Suharto staged a coup, violently suppressed the communist group, and remained in power for six terms (30 years).

Timor Leste gained independence from Portugal, but was then invaded and annexed by Indonesia as its 27th province, before finally being granted independence in 2545 B.E. There were internal security issues due to a rebel group that did not agree with separating from Indonesia. Today, Timor Leste's economy has improved, and it recently became a member of ASEAN.

Brunei

Brunei remained under British protection and became fully independent in 2527 B.E. As a result, Malaysia, Singapore, and Brunei were all able to develop and achieve strong economic growth.

The Philippines

The USA granted independence to the Philippines and supported a democratic government, while the Philippines agreed to let the US establish naval and air force bases in the country. The Philippines operated under its constitution until President Ferdinand Marcos, an elected leader, accumulated too much political power. The US eventually stopped its support, ending his 23-year rule.

5. Civilization Sites and World Heritage in Southeast Asia (pp. 81-91) new

Southeast Asia's long history is evidenced by its many civilization sites. These sites are crucial because they (1) indicate the origin of ethnic groups in the region, and (2) serve as a source of pride for nations, representing long and continuous historical advancement.

Many sites are well preserved for posterity, and some have been registered as World Heritage Sites by UNESCO (the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization). Civilization sites can be grouped into 2 categories: (1) registered as World Cultural Heritage by UNESCO, and (2) not registered.

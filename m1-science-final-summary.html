Chapter 2 — Cells as the Unit of Life

1. Unicellular & Multicellular Organisms

Unicellular organisms consist of one cell only and are also known as microorganisms — very tiny organisms usually living in seas, rivers and lakes, seen only under a microscope. Examples: Chlamydomonas, Paramecium, Euglena, yeast, Amoeba.

Multicellular organisms consist of many cells, each type carrying out specific functions. Man is the most complex multicellular organism. Examples: Hydra, earthworm, Spirogyra, Mucor, grass.

Both types carry out all life processes: movement, response, respiration, digestion, excretion, reproduction and growth.

Organization of cells in multicellular organisms

TissueA group of specialized cells with a similar functionEpithelial tissue

SystemA group of organs working together for a major functionDigestive system / Transportation system

3. General Structure & Functions of Cell Structures

A typical cell has a cell membrane containing protoplasm (nucleus + cytoplasm). The nucleus is spherical, covered by a nucleus membrane. Cytoplasm is a colorless jelly-like material. The mitochondrion produces energy for the cell (plural: mitochondria).

5. Diffusion & Osmosis

Diffusion: movement of molecules from a region of higher to lower concentration. Rate depends on the concentration difference. Examples: smell spreading, teabag in hot water, gas exchange in lungs/leaves.

Osmosis: the net movement of water molecules across a semipermeable membrane from higher to lower water potential. Important for water entering roots, kidney reabsorption, food preservation.

Hypotonic / Isotonic / Hypertonic solutions

Solution typeEffect on cell
Hypotonic (lower solute conc. outside)Water moves in → cell swells, may burst (animal cells lack a wall)
Isotonic (equal conc.)No net water movement → cell stays the same size
Hypertonic (higher solute conc. outside)Water moves out → cell shrinks

Correct order of use

  1. Place the microscope on a flat surface in a well-lit area.
  2. Position the low-power objective lens above the hole of the stage.
  3. Raise the condenser fully and open the diaphragm fully.
  4. Look through the eyepiece; adjust the mirror for a bright field of view.
  5. Place the slide on the stage and secure it with the clip.
  6. Lower the objective lens with the coarse focus knob until just above the slide.
  7. Look through the eyepiece; turn the coarse focus knob to bring the object into focus. Switch to high power if needed.
  8. Adjust the fine focus knob for a sharp image.
Activity — Preparing a slide of human cheek cells: scrape the inside of the cheek gently with the blunt end of a toothpick → place on slide with methylene blue & water → lower cover slip with mounting pin → blot excess with filter paper → observe on low then high power. Caution: do not scrape too hard; do not use dirty toothpicks.
Chapter 3 — Plants

1. Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants (producers) absorb solar energy to make food from carbon dioxide and water; oxygen is released as a by-product. Chlorophyll is the green pigment that traps light energy. Glucose produced is converted to starch and stored in stems, fruits or roots.

Water + Carbon dioxide  —(sunlight / chlorophyll)→  Glucose + Oxygen
6CO₂ + 12H₂O  —(sunlight / chlorophyll)→  C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) + 6O₂ + 6H₂O

Necessary factors: sunlight, chlorophyll, water, carbon dioxide. CO₂ enters through the stomata; the palisade cell (in leaves) is packed with chloroplasts and is the main site of photosynthesis.

2. Vascular Tissue & Transpiration

Vascular tissues transport substances and are equivalent to the mammalian circulatory system: xylem and phloem.

TissueTransportsStructure
XylemWater and mineral salts, roots → stem → leaves; provides supportVessels: elongated dead tubes, walls thickened with lignin, no protoplasm, polygonal, continuous tube
PhloemFood (mainly glucose) between plant partsSieve tubes: living cells with no nucleus, cellulose walls, sieve plates (perforated transverse walls), each with a companion cell

Stem arrangement: Dicot stems — vascular bundles in a ring near the outer edge. Monocot stems — bundles scattered throughout.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the evaporation of water from the aerial parts of plants: 90% lost via stomata, 9% via the cuticle, 1% used in photosynthesis.

Stomata are pores in the leaf/stem epidermis, each bound by a pair of bean-shaped guard cells. Main function: allow gases to diffuse in/out of the leaf. Most stomata open during the day, close at night; they also close when transpiration exceeds water absorption.

FactorEffect on transpiration rate
LightStimulates stomata to open → increases rate
TemperatureWarmer air → faster water movement → increases rate
Relative humidityHigh humidity → reduces water loss
Wind speedHigher wind → carries away water vapor → increases rate
Water availabilityShortage → wilting, stomata close → reduces rate
AltitudeLower atmospheric pressure → increases rate

Roles of transpiration: the pulling force created transports water and minerals through the plant; evaporation cools the leaf surface, protecting delicate cells from sun damage.

3. Sexual Reproductive System of Flowering Plants

The flower sits on the receptacle (expanded end of the flower stalk / pedicel). The outermost parts are the sepals (small, green, leaf-like), protecting the bud. Petals may be colored and scented.

Part of flowerFunction
ReceptacleSupports the flower
SepalsProtect the young flower in the budding stage
PetalsAttract animal pollinators through color and scent
StigmaReceives pollen grains
StyleConnects the stigma to the ovary
OvaryProtects the ovules; develops into the fruit after fertilization
OvuleContains the female gamete (egg); develops into a seed after fertilization
AntherProduces male gametes (in pollen grains)

Stamen (male part) = anther + filament. Pistil / Carpel (female part) = stigma + style + ovary + ovule.

Unisexual flowers contain either stamens (male) or pistils (female) only. Bisexual flowers contain both.

4. Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from anthers to stigmas. Chief agents: wind and insects (mainly bees and butterflies); also birds, bats, snails and water. Pollinators are vital for food, fiber, cooking oil and medicine production — over half the world's fats/oils come from animal-pollinated plants (oil palm, canola, sunflower).

FeatureInsect-pollinatedWind-pollinated
SizeLarge, conspicuousSmall, inconspicuous
PetalsBrightly coloredGreenish/dull
NectariesPresentAbsent
ScentScentedAbsent
AnthersInside the flowerDangle outside, catch the wind

Self-pollination: transfer within the same flower, or between flowers of the same plant. Cross-pollination: transfer from the anther of one plant to the stigma of another plant of the same species.

Examples: insect-pollinated — okra, papaya, sunflower, coconut, durian. Wind-pollinated — wheat, rice, barley, corn, pine.

5. Fertilization, Fruits & Seeds

Fertilization steps: (1) pollen lands on the stigma of the same species → (2) sugary secretion stimulates the pollen to form a pollen tube → (3) the tube grows down the style, digesting tissue as it goes, carrying the male gamete → (4) it enters the ovule through the micropyle → (5) the male gamete fuses with the female gamete (ovum) to form a zygote. One pollen grain fertilizes one ovule only.

After fertilization: sepals, petals and stamens wither and fall off. The fertilized ovule develops into a seed; the ovary develops into the fruit. The fruit wall (from the ovary wall) is the pericarp — hard/dry (e.g. sweet pea, castor oil) or juicy/fleshy (succulent fruits, e.g. mango, tomato).

Structure of a seed

  • Testa — protective covering, prevents drying out
  • Hilum — scar where the seed attached to the ovary wall
  • Micropyle — tiny hole above the hilum, where the pollen tube entered
  • Embryo = Radicle (young root) + Plumule (young shoot) + Cotyledon(s) (seed leaves)
  • Dicotyledonous seeds — 2 cotyledons (e.g. soya bean, broad bean)
  • Monocotyledonous seeds — 1 cotyledon (e.g. paddy, wheat); food stored in the endosperm

Germination

TypeDescriptionExample
EpigealCotyledons rise above the groundMung bean, sunflower
HypogealCotyledons remain below the groundBroad bean, maize

When the testa ruptures, the radicle grows down into the soil and develops root hairs; the plumule grows up and develops the first foliage leaves. Cotyledons shrink as their food reserve is used up.

6. Mineral Requirements in Plants

Plants get carbon, hydrogen and oxygen from CO₂ and water. Other elements (macronutrients: N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) are absorbed from the soil as salts.

MacronutrientEffect of deficiency
Nitrogen (N)Stunted growth; leaves turn yellow and drop
Phosphorus (P)Poor root growth; leaves dark green with red/purple spots
Potassium (K)Premature death; leaves yellow at the edges
Calcium (Ca)Stunted growth; leaves distorted and cupped
Magnesium (Mg)Yellowing between leaf veins; leaves become cupped
Sulphur (S)Young leaves turn yellow and drop

Micronutrients (e.g. boron, ferum, zinc) are needed in much smaller quantities. Fertilizers replace minerals used up by crops, especially when planted closely or over long periods.

7. Asexual Reproduction of Plants

Plant propagation = the process of creating new plants. Sexual propagation (via seeds) produces genetically varied offspring, but takes longer to mature (e.g. lilies take 4–7 years from seed).

Vegetative (asexual) reproduction: a new plant grows from a part of the parent, with the exact genetic make-up as the parent — quality/quantity of flowers, fruits or seeds is known in advance (e.g. lilies take only 2 years to flower this way). Disadvantage: less genetic diversity → offspring less likely to resist pests and diseases.

MethodExamples
BulbsOnions, garlics, lilies, tulips
Underground stemsPotatoes, yams, gingers
